When people hear "an independent child", many picture a child who can manage everything without help. But independence does not come from leaving a child alone with a task. It grows step by step, when a child has room to try things, an adult close by, and the confidence that a mistake is not a problem.

Montessori education often sums this up as "help me to do it myself". That captures the point well: the adult does not do things for the child, but does not leave them without support either.

## Independent does not mean alone

A child who is learning to be independent needs to know they can turn to an adult at any time. The difference is in how we help. Instead of "give it here, I'll do it", try "what could you try first?" or "I'll show you one step and you try the next".

## Prepare the environment

A lot of independence can be supported by how your home is set up:

- a hook and a shelf at the child's height, so they can put away their coat and bag themselves,
- a cup and a small jug they can carry on their own,
- a box of drawing things they can take out and tidy away themselves,
- a clear place for school things that they get ready in the evening.

When things have a fixed place, the child does not have to search and ask every time. And the adult does not have to keep reminding them.

## Choice within limits

Independence does not mean a child does everything their own way. It helps to offer a choice between a few options that are all fine with you. For example: "Do you want to do your homework first, or go outside for half an hour and do it afterwards?" The child learns to make decisions and take responsibility for them, and you know the homework will get done.

## When something goes wrong

Spilt milk, a coat buttoned up wrong, a sum with a mistake. All of this is part of learning. If we shame a child for a mistake, or fix it for them straight away, next time they would rather not try. It helps to calmly say what happened and ask what they will do about it: "The milk has spilt. Where's the cloth?"

The same goes for learning. When a child finds the mistake in a calculation themselves, they learn more than when we underline it for them.

## Examples by age

Every child is different, so take these examples as ideas only:

- **Around 6 to 7:** getting dressed and putting on shoes, making a snack with a little help, putting toys back in their place, watering the plants.
- **Around 8:** getting things ready for the next day, measuring and mixing something from a simple recipe, phoning a grandparent.
- **Around 9 to 10:** planning when to do homework, paying for a small shopping trip, making a simple meal for the whole family.
